Two mappings of the same four variables — population (ratio), temp_c (interval), development_level (ordinal), region (nominal). Hover a point to identify the city.

Visualization 1

Design Justification

I mapped population and temp_c to the x- and y-axes because both are quantitative variables, and position on a common scale is the visual channel humans read most accurately — it lets viewers compare exact magnitudes and spot correlation (or the lack of one) between population and temperature at a glance. Region is nominal with no inherent order, so I used color, a channel with no implied ranking, to group cities categorically. Development_level is ordinal, so I encoded it as size, a channel that has a natural sense of "more/less," matching Low < Medium < High. Together, this lets a viewer ask: are larger (more developed) cities clustered by region, and does population or temperature relate to development level? Color groups regions and size signals development tier without needing to check the legend for every point.

Visualization 2

Design Justification

This version swaps the axes (temp_c on x, population on y) to see whether the same data reads differently when temperature leads the comparison. Development_level, being ordinal, is now mapped to color using a light-to-dark sequential ramp (Low = light, High = dark) so its order stays visible even though color is usually reserved for nominal data — a sequential palette preserves the "more/less" meaning. Region, being nominal, is mapped to shape (circle, square, triangle, diamond) since shape carries no ranking, matching region's lack of order. I expect viewers to notice whether darker (more developed) points cluster at particular temperatures, and whether one shape (region) dominates a corner of the plot — here, development level's gradient is the first thing the eye organizes by.
